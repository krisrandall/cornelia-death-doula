## Sitting with you

The centre of it. I come, I stay a while, and I am not in a hurry. Sometimes we
talk the whole time. Sometimes we don’t talk at all. Either is fine.

## The conversations nobody else will have

What are you frightened of. What do you regret. What have you never told your
children and don’t know how to say. What do you think is coming next.

You can say all of it to me. I am not going to change the subject, and I am not
going to go home and cry about it in a way that then becomes your problem.

## Your story, while you are here to tell it

Being heard is most of it — your story, your fears, your joys, your regrets, and
the legacy you want to leave behind. If you want that recorded, written down or
turned into something for the people who come after you, we can do that too.

## What you want, written down and handed on

A lot of people know exactly how they want their funeral to go and have no idea
how to raise it with their family.

So we talk it through. What music. Where. Who speaks. What you absolutely do not
want. I collate it, and I give it to your family, so that on the worst week of
their lives they are not standing in a room guessing what you would have wanted.

## Knowing what you are actually allowed to do

Most people have no idea how much choice they have. Depending on where you live
in Australia it is often possible to:

- bring someone home from hospital after they have died;
- keep them at home for a few days while people come and say goodbye;
- hold the wake at home, in your own kitchen;
- use a cooling bed or ice mattress instead of a funeral home;
- use a painted shroud, a plain cloth, or a mycelium coffin.

The rules differ from state to state, and I will not guess at yours. Part of my
job is to find out exactly what applies where you are, and to tell you plainly -
including when the answer is no.

## Finding the right people

I don’t want to be the expert on everything, and I won’t pretend to be.

If what you need is to move a parent into aged care, or a palliative care
referral, or a solicitor, or a funeral director who will let you do it your own
way — I will find out who you should be talking to, do the research, and bring
you the information. Then you decide.

## For the people who love you

Families get their own share of this. I can:

- be there in the hours when you cannot be — including when you live too far
  away, and are carrying the guilt of that;
- tell you honestly how they are, so you are not guessing between phone calls;
- sit with you while you work out what to say before it is too late to say it;
- hold the space when everybody’s grief is coming up at once.

## Ceremony, including a living funeral

A living funeral is exactly what it sounds like: the gathering happens while the
person is still here to be in it. People say the things they would otherwise say
over a coffin, to a face.

I have not run one yet, and I will tell you that rather than pretend. If it is
what you want, say so — I know people who do this, I will bring them in, and I
will learn it alongside you. The same goes for marking a threshold that isn’t
death at all: a seventy-fifth birthday, the end of one phase of a life and the
honest start of another.

The offerings will grow as I do.

## Before any of it is urgent

None of this has to wait until someone is actively dying. If you are simply
frightened of death, or curious about it, or want to get your own affairs and
wishes straight while you are well — that is a good reason to come and talk.
That is what the [drop-in hours](/drop-in-hours) are for.

## What it costs

The drop-in hours are free, and always will be. The first proper conversation is
free too — you should be able to find out whether I am the right person without
paying for the privilege.

Beyond that, this is my work and my living, so I charge for it. I will tell you
what something costs before it happens, plainly, and nobody is ever going to get
an invoice they were not expecting. If money is the thing standing in the way,
say so and we will talk about it.
